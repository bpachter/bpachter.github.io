Graph Ontology

A practical guide to giving a business one memory: how to design the ontology, the trust model, and the write-path governance for a knowledge graph that spans many systems, many nodes, and many business domains — and why the ontology, not the database, is the hard part.

Written for architects and engineering leads standing up a graph in an ordinary enterprise — ERP, CRM, contracts, suppliers, customers — not a research lab. Every figure on this page runs live in your tab: toggle it, click it, try to break it.

01

Enterprise amnesia

Every system in a business remembers something, and none of them remember the same thing. The ERP knows a supplier as a vendor number. The CRM knows the same company as an account. The contract repository knows it as a counterparty; the risk system as an exposure; a regulatory registry knows it as a legal entity with an official name. Five systems, five names, five partial memories — and the fact that they describe one real thing lives nowhere except in the heads of the people who work across them.

That is the actual problem a knowledge graph solves. Not search, not visualization — identity and relationship across systems that were never designed to agree. Databases store state: the current row, the latest balance. Knowledge is different. Knowledge is who depends on whom, what was true when, who said so, and how much that source is worth. No operational system holds that, because every operational system was scoped to its own domain on purpose.

The default integration answer is point-to-point: sync the vendor master to the CRM, the CRM to the contract system, and so on. With n systems that is n(n−1)/2 mappings, each one a private, undocumented ontology negotiated between two teams. The graph replaces that mesh with a spine: each system maps once, to a shared identity layer, and every cross-system question becomes a graph traversal instead of a new integration project.

02

The ontology is the contract

An ontology is the answer to two questions, written down before any data arrives: what kinds of things exist, and what kinds of statements about them are legal. It is not documentation of the graph — it is the type system the graph is compiled against. Get it wrong and every downstream consumer inherits the confusion; get it right and the graph can refuse bad data mechanically, without a human in the loop.

Below is a reference ontology for a typical operating business — eight node types and the relations they may participate in. The specific nouns matter less than the properties: every type is disjoint and mechanically decidable, every relation declares what it may connect (and the database enforces it — a CHECK constraint is an ontology law that cannot be forgotten), and anything that fits no declared shape is not "flexible data" — it is a rejected write.

Design rule — types are laws, not labels. A node type must be decidable by code: an organization is a thing that can hold a registration, a contract, an obligation. If two types cannot be told apart mechanically, they are one type. If a "miscellaneous" type appears, the ontology has already failed — it is where meaning goes to die.
Design rule — records are evidence, not structure. Contracts, invoices, filings, and tickets are not nodes. They are the records that edges cite as provenance. Model them as nodes and every document becomes a hairball hub; model them as evidence and every relationship can answer "says who?". The graph holds who-relates-to-whom; the record store holds why you believe it.
03

An edge is a claim, not a fact

The single most consequential design decision in a knowledge-graph memory: edges are claims with provenance, not bare facts. Two statements can look identical in the graph — company A supplies company B — and be worth entirely different amounts. One arrives on a signed contract in your ERP; the other arrives in a press release someone wanted printed. Store them as if they were the same and the graph's value converges to that of its worst source.

So every edge carries its evidence — the source record, the retrieval date, the figure if one exists — and a trust tier assigned by source class, never by how plausible the claim sounds. Plausibility is exactly the property a wrong number is optimized for. Five tiers are enough; the ladder below is a reference taxonomy you can adopt as-is.

Design rule — derive the tier at read time; never store it. Make the tier a pure function of the edge's evidence: its best source class, how it was inferred, and its latest audit verdict. Then attaching one better piece of evidence silently promotes the claim on its next read — no rewrite sweep, no stale labels, and no stored "confidence score" drifting away from measured reality.

Just as important is what happens to claims that fail review: they stay. Nothing is ever deleted from a graph memory — a claim the ontology moved gets a forwarding marker; a claim an audit killed gets a retirement stamp and the auditor's reasoning; a relationship that simply ended gets a supersession date. And a refuted claim becomes a durable negative fact: the next time an extraction pipeline proposes the same pair, it is rejected automatically with the original refutation attached — because "we checked this and it was wrong" is itself knowledge, arguably the most expensive kind. Memory that silently forgets its own corrections will re-learn the same falsehood from the next scrape. New evidence can still beat old memory — but it takes a human to say so.

04

The write path: AI proposes, code verifies, humans publish

LLMs made graph extraction cheap: agents can propose thousands of typed facts a day from contracts, filings, and records. The same property makes ungoverned extraction fatal, because the one thing an LLM must never be able to do is publish a plausible wrong number into the organization's memory. The architecture answer is a division of labor with hard boundaries:

AI proposes. Extraction agents read source documents and emit typed proposals — never writes. Deterministic code verifies. A gate service (deterministic validation wants a compiler and a type system) reconciles each proposal against its own source: extracted figures earn the one passing verdict, publishable, only if they tie to an anchor figure from the same document within a stated tolerance. Failures come back named — undetected_rollup, duplicate_facts, no_consolidated, unreconciled, no_segments — never a bare "failed", because a named failure mode is a routable work item and an unnamed one is a shrug. Humans publish. A review surface (human-in-the-loop curation is a CRUD-and-permissions problem — use the most boring machinery you have) holds the final approve action, and approval is structurally impossible without a passing verdict, three ways at once: the API is read-only (no update endpoint exists that could quietly move a proposal to approved), the approve action refuses with HTTP 409 unless the verdict is publishable, and every gate field is read-only in the review UI so a curator cannot type a verdict into a form. Rejection, meanwhile, is a free bulk action — refusing never needs a verdict. The asymmetry is the whole point.

Close the loop with calibration: every human approve/reject decision becomes ground truth, and a periodically refitted table converts the extractor's raw confidence into what a confidence of this size has empirically been worth. Two implementation rules keep it honest. When there are too few adjudications to fit a meaningful table, refuse to fit one — and when no table exists, the API returns 404 rather than inventing a number. And when a verifier returns no parseable score, defer the proposal rather than stamping a default — a provider outage should leave a queue of honest "not yet judged", never a queue of fabricated 0.5s.

Design rule — absence is honest. A sum that is off by 26% must not publish, even though every row looks reasonable, because a plausible wrong figure is undetectable downstream. The gate's job is not to maximize throughput; it is to make the graph's silence trustworthy.
05

Multi-system, multi-node, multi-domain

One graph, one ontology, one gate — that covers a domain. An enterprise is many domains — procurement, sales, legal, risk, operations — and the architecture has to say precisely what is shared and what is owned.

Shared: the identity spine and the core ontology. Entity identity — which real-world thing is this — is decided exactly once, at a single chokepoint every writer goes through. Anchor it on external identifiers wherever they exist: legal-entity identifiers, company-registry numbers, tax ids — a registry number makes the join deterministic, and name matching becomes a hard gate rather than a score bonus, because two companies sharing a word is how unrelated entities get married. Normalize names for every alphabet your counterparties actually use, or one foreign-language sweep will mint thousands of duplicates. And when normalized names still collide, resolve only to a node that carries an external identifier — one identified candidate is an anchor; zero or two is a refusal. A merge, once made, is a permanent auto-resolution rule for every future write, so the survivor is always chosen on substance — live edges, attached records, identity bridges — never on which name looks cleaner.

Owned: domain extensions. Each domain extends the core ontology with its own types and relations, and owns its own extraction pipelines and source connectors. A domain can evolve its extension without a cross-enterprise change board — but it cannot redefine identity, and it cannot bypass the gate.

The integration surface is a versioned, read-only API — not database access, not a shared warehouse schema. Consumers integrate against the contract, and the graph's internals stay free to change. Writes have exactly one door, and the gate is standing in it.

The pipeline is queued, idempotent, and one-proposal-per-message. A scheduled sweep fans work onto a queue — one message per unit, batch size one, because a batch lets one bad document fail three good ones back into the queue. An extractor proposes, the gate verdicts, and the result lands durably under a deterministic idempotency key — so when (not if) a permissions gap or a crash causes completed work to be redelivered, the redeliveries rewrite one record each instead of duplicating the graph's input. Poison messages dead-letter after three attempts and page a human. And let the verdict taxonomy double as the metrics taxonomy: every named failure mode becomes a dashboard dimension, so the operations view speaks the same language as the code.

Every hop is observable — and observability fails silent by default. Trace the review-surface→gate hop end to end, or the interesting question — how much of my latency is the gate? — has no answer. Then pin your telemetry dependencies like you pin everything else: a wildcard version that silently freezes a trace-id generator produces the worst failure class in distributed systems — a healthy service, a running collector, spans leaving the process, and nothing arriving, with no error on any surface.

06

Recall: serving memory to AI

The graph earns its keep when AI systems start reading from it. Vector retrieval answers what text sounds relevant; the graph answers what is actually connected, according to which source, at what trust tier — and for an agent making decisions, those are different questions with different failure modes. Hallucinated similarity is embarrassing; hallucinated dependency is expensive.

The serving pattern that works is not "give the model the database". Expose a small set of named, typed, bounded query operations — who supplies X, what depends on Y, sole-source components of Z — each with a hard result limit and a uniform envelope. An agent asks one bounded, typed question and gets one compact, high-signal answer, instead of crawling raw tables and inferring types from column names. This is also a cost decision: a legible ontology is a token-economics lever, and the cheapest model call is the one your intent router resolved deterministically for free.

Three guards make graph-backed AI trustworthy. The serving filter is the hallucination guard: apply the same live-edge predicate inside every query tool, so a refuted or retired claim is invisible to the model, not merely unrendered in the UI. Citations are attached out-of-band: every tool records the provenance it touched and the platform renders the sources — the model is never trusted to write its own citations, and web-search results are labeled distinctly so "we searched for this" never reads as "we track this". Access is enforced in code: what the model may reveal is set by the authenticated session, never by anything said in the conversation — a request to "ignore that" changes nothing, because there is nothing at the prompt layer to ignore.

And close this loop too: audit a rolling sample of served claims, and let the results re-price the writers. A writer — human pipeline or model — whose confirmed/refuted record falls below par gets an absolute cap on the confidence it can assert, and a fixed golden set of adjudicated claims separates real improvement from sampling luck. The claims a change kills that the golden set expected to survive are the most important list your quality process produces.

Design rule — memory is the eval. The same provenance discipline that governs writes gives you the evaluation harness for free: any AI answer that cites the graph can be checked mechanically against the cited claims. The graph is not just what the AI knows — it is how you measure whether the AI is lying.
07

The build order

Sequencing is most of the risk. The failure pattern is building the impressive parts first — extraction agents, chat interfaces — on top of an identity layer that does not exist yet. The order that works, each step producing something inspectable before the next begins:

Identity spine

One node per real-world entity, external identifiers as anchors, every system's local keys mapped onto it, and a merge procedure with an audit trail. Boring, decisive, first. Every later step assumes it.

Core ontology, versioned

The types and relations every domain must share, written as schema-enforceable laws with a version number. Small is correct: a core ontology under ten types is a feature, not a gap — domains will extend it.

First domain, end to end

One domain's sources flowing through extraction to published, provenance-carrying edges — before any second domain starts. The first domain debugs the architecture; the second debugs the federation.

The gate

Deterministic verification with named failure modes, with the failure taxonomy unit-tested as the spec. From this point forward nothing reaches the graph unverified — including backfills.

Adjudication

The human approval surface, with approval structurally impossible without a passing verdict. Reviewers see the proposal, its source, its verdict, and its history — and their decisions feed calibration.

The flywheel

Queued extraction at volume, now that every proposal it emits meets a gate it cannot pass by being plausible. Scale the proposers only after the refusal machinery is proven.

Serving & evals

The versioned read API, typed query operations for agents, trust tiers surfaced in every answer — and the graph doubling as the evaluation harness for every AI system that cites it.

The standards to write down on day one, because retrofitting them is an archaeology project: the identity-merge procedure, the ontology versioning policy, the trust-tier taxonomy, the named failure-mode list, and the rule that no consumer ever integrates below the API. Five documents. Each one is a page. Together they are the difference between a knowledge graph and a very expensive cache of unverified strings.
