Part III

The Picture

Assembling the evidence into the modern map: the continent of galaxies we belong to, what "empty" really means, and why the cheap seats might be the good ones.

08 ·

Laniakea

Part II left us with a puzzle shaped like an answer: everything around us drifts toward one point, and behind the dust there really is a massive core. So is the Great Attractor a thing — one monstrous object reeling us in? In 2014, a team led by R. Brent Tully proposed a better question. Take every galaxy whose motion we've measured, subtract the expansion, and ask of each one: where does its stream ultimately drain? Group galaxies by shared destination — the way geographers group rivers into watersheds — and our home structure simply appears.

They named it Laniakea — Hawaiian for "immense heaven," honoring the Polynesian navigators who read the sky to cross an ocean. It is a basin of attraction some 500 million light-years across containing the motion of roughly 100,000 large galaxies, and the Great Attractor region is simply its lowest point — the drain at the bottom of our watershed. The Milky Way sits far from the center, out near the rim, where our stream begins its long fall inward.

← So the Attractor is less "an object pulling us" and more "the place everything here is falling toward." The name survives because it's a great name.

A two-basin velocity field with the honest caveats: the flow doesn't stop at the Attractor — Shapley, the heaviest concentration in the nearby universe, tugs from beyond it, and the Dipole Repeller (an enormous void mapped in 2017) pushes from behind. Our 600 km/s is the sum of pulls ahead and a push at our backs. And the borders are still being redrawn — some recent work folds Laniakea into an even larger basin draining toward Shapley.
Laniakea is a watershed of gravity: 100,000 galaxies defined not by where they sit but by where they're going. The Great Attractor is its drain — and we live out by the rim, mid-fall.
09 ·

Emptier holes

Chapter 01 owed you a correction, and here it is. We've been calling the KBC region a "void," and by the standards of true voids, that's slander. The gold standard of nothing is the Boötes void — the "Great Nothing" — discovered in 1981: a cavity around 330 million light-years across where surveys expected on the order of two thousand galaxies and have found roughly sixty. Three percent occupancy. If the Milky Way lived there, we might not have known other galaxies existed until deep into the space age.

Now hold that against our own address. The KBC underdensity is vastly larger — around two billion light-years — but only modestly thin: something like 20 to 80 percent of average density depending on where you stand in it, not three percent. It contains most of Laniakea, our supercluster, everything in Parts I and II. Underdense is not empty. We don't live in a desert; we live in a thinly settled countryside.

← And the thinness may matter: a genuinely underdense neighborhood would make locally-measured expansion look a touch fast — one proposed ingredient in the Hubble tension from chapter 01. Actively debated; most analyses say it can't be the whole story.

Slide from "average universe" to "what's actually there" and watch both holes empty out — one barely, one almost completely.

10 ·

The suburb

So here is your completed address, each line now earned: a quiet arm of the Milky Way → a galaxy falling toward Andromeda → the Local Group → out near the rim of Laniakea, mid-fall toward the Great Attractor → all of it inside a thinly settled region two billion light-years wide. Every line of that address says the same thing: we live in the suburbs — of the galaxy, of the supercluster, of the neighborhood itself.

Is that a demotion? Consider what the dense districts are actually like. Cluster cores are crowded with galaxies swinging past one another, stripping each other's gas, feeding central black holes that floodlight their surroundings in radiation, and popping off supernovae in close quarters. Busy, bright — and hard on delicate, slow projects. Projects like, say, four billion years of uninterrupted biochemistry.

← Or, in the other direction: maybe everyone interesting is downtown at the Attractor and we're just very far from the party. The data does not currently distinguish these possibilities.

Compare the neighborhoods yourself — and mind the label on the box below, because this chapter's charm exceeds its certainty.

Interactions scale super-linearly with crowding — pack more galaxies in and the disruption rate climbs faster than the count. The suburb just sits there, quietly compounding its billions of calm years.
speculation, clearly labeledThe idea that galactic suburbs favor life — a "galactic habitable zone" writ large — is a real hypothesis discussed in the literature, not a result. The honest version: dense environments are demonstrably more disruptive, life's tolerance for disruption is unknown, sample size = 1, and the one known sample lives in the suburbs. Make of that exactly as much as it deserves.
Underdense, off-center, mid-fall — and possibly better off for all three. The suburbs of the universe are not where the action is. They may be where the time is.

The neighborhood is mapped. But every map in Parts I–III lived inside a bigger question we kept deferring: how far can seeing reach at all? Why is the night black when the universe is full of light? What actually sits at the edge of everything we can observe — and how does 13.8-billion-year-old light arrive from 46 billion light-years away? Part IV · The Bubble takes on the edge of seeing itself — and Part V · The Bang goes back to the beginning that happened everywhere at once, including exactly where you're sitting.
