Book II · Part VII — the finale

The business

Everything converges here: Book I's agents, gates, and fleets running on Book II's graph. This is what an AI-native company actually looks like from the inside — and how to build one without burning it down.

27 ·

The flywheel

Put the pieces together and something new happens: the graph starts growing itself. Every night, extractor agents (the ch-11 loop, pointed at documents) read filings and propose candidate edges. Verifier agents — skeptics by prompt, like the ch-15 crew — check each proposal against sources and stamp it. And the ch-14 blast-radius gate decides what applies on its own: a normal night's trickle flows through; a suspicious burst freezes and pages a human.

The compounding is the point. Each verified edge makes tomorrow's extraction smarter (more context to anchor against), each rejection tunes the verifiers, and the graph gets richer while you sleep — with receipts on every edge (ch 25), because an unverified flywheel is just a misinformation compounder. Watch a few nights run:

A few nights on the flywheel
nights 0edges verified 0rejected 0verifier accepts ~70%

Try it → Dashed = proposed, green = verified, faded = rejected. Drag strictness: too loose and unchecked edges compound into a misinformation engine; too strict and the flywheel starves, rejecting good facts with the bad. When the extractor floods the queue, the gate freezes the whole batch — and you hold the release.

28 ·

GraphRAG

Remember chapter 10h: RAG fetches text chunks that sound like the question. That works until the answer doesn't live in any single document. "Fab-9 flooded — which of our customers are exposed?" is a two-hop question: the flood memo mentions Fab-9, a supplier contract links Fab-9 to Chipco, a sales record links Chipco to your customers. No chunk contains the chain. Similarity search retrieves three documents about flooding and answers with confident irrelevance.

GraphRAG retrieves structure instead: land the question on a node, walk the typed edges (the ch-22 walk), and hand the model the subgraph — the actual dependency chain, with provenance attached. The answer arrives with an edge trail for a citation. Same question, both retrievers:

"Fab-9 flooded — who's exposed?"

Notice → Chunk-RAG finds documents that mention flooding. GraphRAG finds the blast radius — and its citation is the path itself.

29 ·

The model fleet

Running everything through a frontier model is like commuting by helicopter. A real operation runs a fleet: small local models (quantized, ch 10g, effectively $0 per token) grind the bulk work — extraction, embedding, tagging; mid-size verifiers check what the extractors propose; and the frontier model is reserved for what actually needs judgment — adjudicating conflicts, writing the brief a human will read. A router assigns each task to the cheapest tier that can do it well.

The economics are not subtle. Watch a night's task list route through the fleet, and what the same list would have cost helicopter-style:

30 ·

Humans in the org chart

The last design question isn't technical — it's organizational: where do the people go? The answer from ch 14, scaled up: humans move from doing the work to holding the gates — setting the bounds, taking the escalations, auditing the ledger. Every action an agent takes is logged with its reasoning and its receipts; every escalation lands on a person with full context; and the delegation level is a dial you turn deliberately, one notch at a time, as trust is earned.

Turn the dial yourself. Watch what each level costs in interruptions — and what full autonomy quietly gives up:

Try it → At L1 you approve everything and nothing moves. At L4 nothing asks — note the caution in the ledger. L3 is where real systems live: bounded autonomy, receipts for everything.

31 ·

The modern data department

Put the whole book on one screen. Sources flow through one governed pipeline into a typed, receipted graph. An agent flywheel orbits it — extracting, verifying, curating — behind gates a human holds. And decisions come off the far side: screeners, briefs, alerts, every one traceable back through the graph to a source. No spreadsheet empires, no data swamps, no dashboard over quicksand — the hall of shame, retired.

Read it left to right → Sources → one pipeline → the graph, with the agent flywheel orbiting it → decisions with receipts. Hover any box to see what it does and which chapter it comes from. Every chapter of both books is somewhere on this screen.

And if this is Monday morning and you're starting from a hall-of-shame stack, the path is shorter than it looks:

  • 1 · Pick the system of record for each fact — and kill the copies
  • 2 · Write the ontology: the 12 nouns and 8 verbs your business runs on
  • 3 · One pipeline in, with lineage and freshness alarms
  • 4 · Agents propose, verifiers check, a gate applies — never raw writes
  • 5 · Dashboards and briefs read the graph. Nothing else. Ever.
