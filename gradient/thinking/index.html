Part V — the finale

Thinking

The newest chapter of the story: models that pause, reason, and check themselves — how that skill is trained, what it costs, and what we find when we finally look inside.

17 ·

Thinking out loud

Here's a question with a trap in it: a bat and a ball cost $1.10 together. The bat costs $1.00 more than the ball. How much is the ball? Your gut says ten cents — quickly, confidently, wrongly. Language models inherited the same failure, because next-token prediction (chapter 08) is a gut: one forward pass, one blurted answer.

The fix turned out to be almost embarrassing: let the model talk to itself first. Ask it to reason step by step — chain of thought — and every intermediate line it writes becomes context that constrains the next one. The scratchpad isn't decoration; it's computation happening in text. Modern reasoning models take this further with dedicated thinking tokens — sometimes thousands of them, hidden or shown — before a single word of answer. Same network, radically better arithmetic, logic, and planning.

Below, one model, two habits. Blurt mode answers the bat-and-ball instantly, twenty times. Scratchpad mode writes out the algebra first, twenty times. Watch the tallies diverge.

18 ·

Training a reasoner

You can't teach good reasoning the chapter-09 way alone — humans voting on vibes can't tell a lucky answer from a sound one. But math and code have a superpower: the answer is checkable. So the modern recipe is RLVR — reinforcement learning from verifiable rewards: let the model try problems, pay it only when a verifier confirms the result, and let it discover for itself that careful habits earn more than confident guessing.

The algorithm that made this cheap is GRPO — group relative policy optimization. Sample a group of, say, eight attempts at the same problem. Score them all. The group's average becomes the baseline: attempts above it get reinforced, attempts below it get suppressed. No separate value network to train (the heavyweight machinery classic PPO drags around) — the group grades itself on a curve. Run the rounds below and watch the strategy distribution migrate toward "work it out, then check it."

19 ·

The thinking budget

Training compute used to be the whole story. Reasoning models opened a second dial: compute at answer time. Given a hard question, you can spend tokens three ways — think longer (one deeper chain), think wider (many independent chains, then majority vote — self-consistency), or think wider with a judge (generate N candidates, let a verifier pick — best-of-N). Accuracy climbs with spend on all three curves… until it doesn't. Every curve flattens; the skill is knowing where yours bends.

This is why the model you talk to sometimes answers instantly and sometimes visibly deliberates: a router is deciding, per question, how much thinking your question deserves. Drag the budget below and watch the three strategies trade places.

Try it → At small budgets, one deep chain wins. Push the budget and voting overtakes it — independent mistakes cancel. A verifier squeezes out the last few points, at the price of trusting the judge.

20 ·

Inside the mind

One mystery is left: what's actually in there? The naive hope — one neuron, one concept — dies immediately. A model has vastly more ideas than neurons, so it does the only thing it can: superposition, packing many concepts into shared neurons at odd angles, like six voices sharing two microphones. Peer at a single neuron and you see gibberish — a little dog, a little bridge, a little arithmetic.

The tool that un-mixes the voices is the sparse autoencoder — a lens trained to re-describe the tangled activations in a much larger dictionary of features, only a few active at a time. Through the SAE lens, the mush resolves into legible ideas. And features aren't just visible — they're handles: dial one up and the model's behavior follows it, famously demonstrated when researchers cranked a "Golden Gate Bridge" feature and the model couldn't stop bringing it up. Interpretability is young, but this is the path: not reading minds, but finding the dials inside one.
