Language

How words become numbers, how attention lets every word watch every other word, and the one-skill loop that writes whole essays.

05 ·

Words become numbers

Networks eat numbers, not words. So language models first chop text into tokens — not quite letters, not quite words. Common words survive whole; rare ones get split into reusable pieces, so unbelievable becomes un + believ + able and the model can handle words it has never seen. Every model has a fixed menu of these pieces, learned by merging the most frequent pairs — the reason models sometimes struggle to count the letters in a word: they never see letters, only chunks.

Then each token gets a meaning. Not a definition — a location. Every token becomes an embedding: a list of hundreds of coordinates placing it in a vast space where similar meanings sit near each other. The astonishing part, discovered with word2vec in 2013, is that directions in this space mean things. The path from man to woman is roughly the same arrow as the path from king to queen. Meaning becomes geometry, and arithmetic on meaning becomes possible.

06 ·

Attention is all you need

For years, models read sentences one word at a time, dragging a fading memory behind them — and long sentences dissolved into fog. The 2017 transformer threw that out with one move: let every word look at every other word at once, and let the model learn where to look. That mechanism is attention, and it is the single idea behind the entire modern AI boom.

Each word plays three roles at once. Its query asks a question — what am I looking for? Its key advertises what it offers. Its value is the actual information it hands over. Every query is compared against every key; strong matches earn strong weights; and each word rebuilds itself as a weighted blend of everyone's values. When you read the robot picked up the ball because it was heavy, something in you resolves what it means. Attention is that reflex, made mechanical.

who is looking at whom?

Try it → Tap any word to see where its attention flows. Then switch on the causal mask — in a generator, no word may peek at the future, so everything after the chosen word goes dark.

Because all words are seen at once, the model needs a sense of order — so each position gets a rhythmic stamp built from sine and cosine waves (newer models rotate the query and key vectors instead, a scheme called RoPE — it gets its own chapter in part III).

07 ·

Inside the tower, in 3-D

One attention pass is a committee meeting. A transformer is dozens of them, stacked. Each layer runs several heads in parallel — small independent spotlights, each free to learn its own kind of relationship: one watches the previous word, one binds verbs to their objects, one hunts for the far-away noun a pronoun leans on, one keeps an eye on everything at once.

Layer by layer, every word's vector is rebuilt as a blend of what it attended to — meaning refined in passes, the way a drawing goes sketch → inks → color. By the top of the tower, the vector over ball doesn't just mean "ball"; it means this particular ball, the one the robot picked up. Below is a toy tower you can hold: six words wide, four layers tall, four heads per layer.

08 ·

The autocomplete machine

Here is the secret that still surprises people: a language model has exactly one skill. Given everything so far, it predicts a probability for every possible next token. That's it. Generation is just prediction on a loop — predict, pick one, append it, predict again — each new word feeding the next prediction. The loop is why answers stream out word by word, and why a model never quite knows how its own sentence will end.

How you pick from those probabilities is a personality dial. Always take the single most likely token — greedy decoding — and the text turns repetitive and dull. Instead we sample. Temperature reshapes the whole distribution: cold makes the favorite dominate, hot flattens the field toward chaos. Top-k only lets the k best candidates into the draw. Top-p keeps the smallest set of words whose probabilities add up to p. Every chatbot you've used is these three sliders wearing a trench coat.

The model below is real and it learned from this very page — every probability you see comes from counting patterns in the words you just read. It is a humble n-gram model, not a transformer, but the sampling machinery is exactly what the big models use. Watch the outlined bars (raw probabilities) get reshaped into the filled bars (after your dials) at every single step.
