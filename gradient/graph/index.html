Book II · Part VI

The graph

Book I taught how the machine thinks. Book II is about the other half of running a business on AI: how you store what your company knows — and why nodes and edges beat rows and columns the moment relationships matter.

21 ·

Everything is a record

Strip any company to the studs and you find the same thing: facts about things and the relationships between them. Acme buys from Beta. Beta makes alloy frames. The contract renews in March. Every architecture ever invented is just a different costume for those facts — and each costume makes some questions easy and others miserable.

The five costumes worth knowing: relational tables (facts as rows, relationships exiled into join tables), documents (facts nested inside each other, duplicated wherever needed), key-value (facts behind opaque locker doors, blazing fast if you know the key), columnar (facts sliced vertically, built for adding up a billion numbers), and the graph (things as nodes, relationships as first-class, typed edges). Below, one tiny supply chain wearing all five. Watch the gold fact — Beta supplies Acme — change costume.

notice → none of these is wrong. columnar crushes "sum all invoices"; key-value crushes "fetch cart #4512". the question is what your hardest questions look like — and a business's hardest questions are almost always about relationships.

22 ·

The join tax

Here's the question that finds the crack in the relational world: "who supplies the suppliers of my suppliers?" One hop is a JOIN. Two hops is a JOIN of a JOIN. Every extra hop multiplies the rows the database must examine, because relational storage doesn't store relationships — it re-derives them, at query time, every time, by matching keys across tables.

A graph database makes the opposite bet: pay for the relationship once, at write time, storing each edge as a physical pointer. Then a three-hop question is just three pointer-walks from the starting node — the cost grows with the size of the answer, not the size of the database. That single design decision is why supply chains, fraud rings, org charts, and knowledge graphs — anything where the question is "what's connected to what" — live on graphs.

honesty → indexes tame the worst of this, and relational is still the right home for plenty of data. the point is the shape: joins recompute relationships; graphs simply keep them.

23 ·

Nodes & edges, done right

A graph won't save you from bad modeling — it just makes bad modeling more visible. The craft has a few iron rules. Things become nodes; relationships become edges; both get types. An edge labeled supplies with a quantity and a start date is knowledge; an edge labeled related_to is a shrug in database form. Direction matters — Beta supplies Acme is not Acme supplies Beta. And not every fact deserves a node: an address is a property; a Vietnam-war-surplus warehouse full of one-off nodes is clutter wearing a graph costume.

24 ·

The ontology

Types alone aren't enough — you need a vocabulary contract: which node classes exist (Organization, Program, Part), how they nest (a Subsidiary is an Organization), and what's forbidden (a Part can't employ a Person). That contract is the ontology, and it's what turns a pile of edges into something agents and queries can reason over without guessing what words mean.

The hardest clause in the contract is identity. The wild world will hand you "Northrop Grumman Corp," "NORTHROP GRUMMAN SYSTEMS CORPORATION," and "Northrop Grumman Space Systems" — and if each becomes its own node, your graph quietly shatters into fragments that know nothing about each other. Entity resolution is the discipline of merging what's truly the same and — just as important — refusing to merge what merely looks the same. Ticker collisions, shared surnames, single-token names: the traps are everywhere. Your turn on the merge desk:

25 ·

Provenance, or receipts

An enterprise graph that agents write into (that's part IV's whole premise) has one non-negotiable rule: no edge without a receipt. Every relationship carries its sources, each source carries a trust tier — a government contract filing outranks a press release, which outranks a model's inference — and the edge's confidence is computed from its evidence, never typed in by hand.

Receipts change how truth dies, too. When a new filing contradicts an edge, you don't delete history — you mark the edge disputed and keep both records, because next quarter's question might be "what did we believe in March, and why?" That's bitemporal thinking: the graph remembers not just what's true, but when it was known. Try it on one edge:
