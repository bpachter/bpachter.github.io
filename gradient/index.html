01 ·

Hello, neuron

Your brain runs on about eighty six billion tiny cells. Each one listens to signals arriving on its dendrites, adds them up in the cell body, and — if the total crosses a threshold — fires a pulse down its axon to thousands of neighbors. Learning, in the brain, is just certain connections getting stronger with use.

In 1958 Frank Rosenblatt asked a beautiful question: what if we build that in math? His perceptron takes numbers in, multiplies each by a weight, adds a bias, and fires if the weighted sum crosses zero. That single multiply-and-add is still the atom of every neural network today. A weight is just a dial that says how much should I care about this input?

02 ·

Learning is descending

How does a machine find good weights on its own? First we give it a score to hate: a loss function that measures how wrong the current weights are. Wrong predictions mean high loss. Perfect predictions mean zero. Now learning has a shape — a landscape of hills and valleys where altitude is error — and training becomes a search for the lowest valley.

The search trick is called gradient descent, and it is almost embarrassingly simple. Stand anywhere on the landscape. Feel the slope under your feet — that slope is the gradient. Take a small step downhill. Repeat. The size of the step is the learning rate, and it is the most temperamental dial in machine learning: too small and training takes forever, too large and you leap clean over the valley and ricochet up the far wall.

Ball below. Loss curve below it. See for yourself — and notice the local valley that isn't the deepest one. Real networks dodge such traps partly by taking noisy steps, a trick called stochastic gradient descent: cheaper, jitterier, and strangely better at finding the deep valleys.

03 ·

Going deep

One neuron draws one straight line — and straight lines have limits. In 1969 Minsky and Papert pointed at a toddler-simple puzzle the perceptron provably cannot solve: XOR, where the answer is yes for one of two inputs but not both. The pattern is diagonal. No single line separates it. Funding dried up, and the field entered its first winter.

The thaw came from stacking. Put neurons in layers, feed the outputs of one layer into the next, and squeeze each neuron through a curved activation function so the whole thing stops being one big linear equation. Then train every dial at once with backpropagation — the chain rule from calculus, walking the error backward through the network, handing each weight its own personal share of the blame.

The curve you choose matters more than it looks. The classic sigmoid squashes everything between zero and one — but its slopes are shallow, and multiplying many small slopes together makes the error signal vanish before it reaches the early layers. Deep networks stalled for years on exactly this. The modern fix is almost comically blunt: ReLU, which keeps positive numbers and zeroes the rest. Cheap, crude, and it trains beautifully.

This one is fully live: a real two-layer network, trained by real backpropagation, in your browser, on the very problem that started the winter. Watch the painted decision region bend around the diagonal — something no single neuron could ever do. Then switch activations and feel the difference in training speed.

04 ·

Words become numbers

Networks eat numbers, not words. So language models first chop text into tokens — not quite letters, not quite words. Common words survive whole; rare ones get split into reusable pieces, so unbelievable becomes un + believ + able and the model can handle words it has never seen. Every model has a fixed menu of these pieces, learned by merging the most frequent pairs — the reason models sometimes struggle to count the letters in a word: they never see letters, only chunks.

Then each token gets a meaning. Not a definition — a location. Every token becomes an embedding: a list of hundreds of coordinates placing it in a vast space where similar meanings sit near each other. The astonishing part, discovered with word2vec in 2013, is that directions in this space mean things. The path from man to woman is roughly the same arrow as the path from king to queen. Meaning becomes geometry, and arithmetic on meaning becomes possible.

05 ·

Attention is all you need

For years, models read sentences one word at a time, dragging a fading memory behind them — and long sentences dissolved into fog. The 2017 transformer threw that out with one move: let every word look at every other word at once, and let the model learn where to look. That mechanism is attention, and it is the single idea behind the entire modern AI boom.

Each word plays three roles at once. Its query asks a question — what am I looking for? Its key advertises what it offers. Its value is the actual information it hands over. Every query is compared against every key; strong matches earn strong weights; and each word rebuilds itself as a weighted blend of everyone's values. When you read the robot picked up the ball because it was heavy, something in you resolves what it means. Attention is that reflex, made mechanical.

who is looking at whom?

try it → tap any word to see where its attention flows. then switch on the causal mask — in a generator, no word may peek at the future, so everything after the chosen word goes dark.

Two footnotes make the machine whole. Because all words are seen at once, the model needs a sense of order — so each position gets a rhythmic stamp built from sine and cosine waves (newer models rotate the query and key vectors instead, a scheme called RoPE). And attention runs many times in parallel — multi-head — each head free to learn its own kind of relationship: one tracks grammar, another tracks pronouns, another tracks rhyme. Stack attention and small feed-forward networks a few dozen layers high, and you have a transformer.

06 ·

The autocomplete machine

Here is the secret that still surprises people: a language model has exactly one skill. Given everything so far, it predicts a probability for every possible next token. That's it. Generation is just prediction on a loop — predict, pick one, append it, predict again — each new word feeding the next prediction. The loop is why answers stream out word by word, and why a model never quite knows how its own sentence will end.

How you pick from those probabilities is a personality dial. Always take the single most likely token — greedy decoding — and the text turns repetitive and dull. Instead we sample. Temperature reshapes the whole distribution: cold makes the favorite dominate, hot flattens the field toward chaos. Top-k only lets the k best candidates into the draw. Top-p keeps the smallest set of words whose probabilities add up to p. Every chatbot you've used is these three sliders wearing a trench coat.

The model below is real and it learned from this very essay — every probability you see comes from counting patterns in the words you just read. It is a humble n-gram model, not a transformer, but the sampling machinery is exactly what the big models use. 07 ·

Sculpting the mind

Pre-training on the internet produces a brilliant autocomplete with no manners. Turning it into an assistant takes two more acts. First, fine-tuning on curated instruction-and-answer examples. But retraining billions of weights for every new task is absurd — so we cheat, elegantly. LoRA freezes the giant weight matrices and learns only two skinny side-matrices whose product nudges the original. Same behavior change, a fraction of a percent of the parameters — a lightweight plug-in for a mind.

Second act: alignment. Show the model two of its own answers and tell it which one a human preferred. Classic RLHF trains a separate reward model and optimizes against it with reinforcement learning (PPO). The newer, cleaner DPO skips the middleman — a bit of algebra on the Bradley-Terry preference model turns "A beats B" directly into a training loss. Thousands of tiny votes like yours, below, are what taught chatbots to be helpful instead of merely fluent.

My course notes end in early 2025 — but the field didn't. A last page of field notes, for what the frontier looks like now:

Mixture of Experts

Instead of one giant brain, dozens of specialist sub-networks. A router sends each token to just 2 of 8+ experts — huge capacity, small compute bill. Mixtral, Qwen3-MoE, and most frontier models now.

KV cache

During generation the model stores every past token's keys and values so each new token only pays for itself. The memory hog of inference — and the thing your context window is really made of.

RoPE

Rotary position embeddings replace sine-wave stamps by rotating query & key vectors by an angle per position. Relative distances fall out naturally; long contexts extrapolate better.

GQA / MQA

Grouped-query attention: many query heads share a few key/value heads. Nearly the same quality, a fraction of the KV-cache memory — how models afford 128K contexts.

FlashAttention

The same attention math, reorganized to live in fast GPU memory tiles instead of thrashing slow memory. Not an approximation — just better bookkeeping. 2-4× faster training.

Speculative decoding

A small draft model races ahead writing cheap tokens; the big model verifies them in one parallel pass, keeping the correct prefix. Same output, much faster.

Quantization

Weights stored in 4-8 bits instead of 16 (GGUF, AWQ). How a 30B-parameter model runs on a single RTX 4090 — the trick behind every local LLM.

RAG

Retrieval-augmented generation: embed your documents, fetch the relevant chunks, staple them into the prompt. The model stays frozen; the knowledge stays fresh.
