Part III

The engine room

How a raw autocomplete becomes an assistant — and the engine-room tricks that make modern AI fast enough, and cheap enough, to feel instant.

09 ·

Sculpting the mind

Pre-training on the internet produces a brilliant autocomplete with no manners. Turning it into an assistant takes two more acts. First, fine-tuning on curated instruction-and-answer examples. But retraining billions of weights for every new task is absurd — so we cheat, elegantly. LoRA freezes the giant weight matrices and learns only two skinny side-matrices whose product nudges the original. Same behavior change, a fraction of a percent of the parameters — a lightweight plug-in for a mind.

the LoRA trick, to scale
full fine-tune 16.8M params LoRA 65.5K params that's 0.39%
7B model, FP16
≈ 98 GB to train
7B with QLoRA
≈ 24.5 GB — one gaming GPU

and → QLoRA shrinks the frozen weights to 4-bit — like reducing a photo from 256 shades of gray to 16: smaller, still recognizable.

Second act: alignment. Show the model two of its own answers and tell it which one a human preferred. Classic RLHF trains a separate reward model and optimizes against it with reinforcement learning (PPO). The newer, cleaner DPO skips the middleman — a bit of algebra on the Bradley-Terry preference model turns "A beats B" directly into a training loss. Thousands of tiny votes like yours, below, are what taught chatbots to be helpful instead of merely fluent.

you are the reward signal

10 ·

The engine room

Everything above explains what a model knows. This chapter is about why talking to one feels instant instead of glacial. Eight ideas run the show — every one of them working, animated, below. This is the machinery humming under Claude, ChatGPT, and the local model on my own GPU.

10a ·Mixture of Experts — don't wake the whole brain

A dense model spends every neuron on every token, which is like convening the entire company to answer one email. A Mixture of Experts model keeps a stable of specialist sub-networks and a tiny learned router that picks just two of them per token. You get a brain with enormous capacity where only a sliver does work at any moment — Mixtral, Qwen3-MoE, and most frontier models are built this way.

10b ·The KV cache — never re-read the conversation

Attention compares every new token against every token before it. Done naively, the model would re-derive keys and values for the whole conversation on every single word — quadratic pain, forever. The KV cache is the fix: compute each token's keys and values once, keep them, and let each new token pay only for itself.

It is also the memory hog of inference. Your "context window" is, physically, this cache sitting in GPU memory — which is why long chats are expensive and why so much of part III is about shrinking it.

10c ·RoPE — position as a clock hand

Attention is order-blind, so position has to be smuggled in. The old way stamped each slot with sine waves. The modern way — rotary position embeddings — rotates each query and key vector by an angle proportional to its position, like giving every word a clock hand.

The payoff is subtle and huge: when two rotated vectors meet in attention, their dot product depends only on the angle between them — the relative distance — not on where the pair sits in the essay. Slide the pair below and watch both hands turn while the angle between them refuses to change.

10d ·GQA — eight questioners, two filing cabinets

Every attention head keeping its own private keys and values multiplies the KV cache by the head count — a memory tax that decides how long your context can be. Grouped-query attention lets many query heads share a few key/value heads: eight question-askers consulting two shared filing cabinets. Nearly the same quality, a fraction of the cache — a big part of how models afford 128K-token contexts.

10e ·FlashAttention — the same math, arranged politely

The attention score matrix between every pair of words is enormous, and the slow part isn't computing it — it's writing it to memory. GPUs have a tiny, blazing-fast scratchpad (SRAM) and a huge, slow warehouse (HBM). Naive attention builds the whole matrix in the warehouse. FlashAttention computes it in small tiles that never leave the scratchpad, streaming a running answer out and materializing nothing.

It is not an approximation. Same numbers, exact to the bit — just better bookkeeping, worth 2-4× in training speed. Watch both strategies process the same matrix:

watch → naive fills the whole square in slow memory. flash chews tiles in the scratchpad and writes only the skinny answer column.

10f ·Speculative decoding — a fast intern, a careful editor

Big models write slowly because each token is one full pass through the tower. So pair them: a tiny draft model sprints ahead and types a few cheap tokens; the big model then checks all of them in a single parallel pass, keeps the correct prefix, and fixes the first mistake with its own word. Provably the same output distribution as the big model alone — just two to three times faster. Free speed.

10g ·Quantization — fewer shades of gray

Every weight is just a stored number — and you choose how many bits to spend on it. 16-bit is studio quality. Squeeze to 4 bits and each weight must snap to one of sixteen shelves: the model gets slightly gritty and four times smaller. This is GGUF and AWQ, the trick behind every local LLM — how a 30-billion-parameter model fits on a single RTX 4090.

10h ·RAG — a frozen mind with a fresh library

A model's knowledge ends at its training day, and retraining is expensive. Retrieval-augmented generation bolts on a library instead: chop your documents into chunks, embed each one into the meaning-space from chapter 05, and when a question arrives, fish out the nearest chunks and staple them into the prompt. The model stays frozen; the knowledge stays fresh; the answer comes with receipts.
